01 · Overview & Population Snapshot
This dataset captures 5,190 Australian patients surveyed about their doctor visit behaviour, demographics, health status, and insurance coverage. The central finding is immediately striking: nearly 4 in 5 patients recorded zero doctor visits in the reference period, producing a heavily zero-inflated count distribution that demands specialised modelling (zero-inflated Poisson or negative-binomial regression). The analysis that follows peels back every layer — from who visits most, to why, to what systemic factors drive utilisation.

1 · Doctor Visits Distribution
The distribution is severely right-skewed. 79.8% of patients have 0 visits; only 782 (15%) visited once. Frequencies drop sharply thereafter — a textbook zero-inflated count pattern.
2 · Cumulative Visit % (CDF)
94.9% of patients have ≤1 visit and 98.2% have ≤2. This cumulative view confirms that "heavy users" (3+ visits) are a very small but high-cost minority.
3 · Gender Split
The sample is 52% female, reflecting typical survey demographics and consistent with higher female healthcare engagement.
02 · Health Burden — Illness, Chronic Conditions & Health Score
Health burden is the single most powerful predictor of doctor visits in this dataset. Patients with severe illness (score 5) visit at a rate 10× higher than those with no illness. Chronic conditions — particularly limiting ones — dramatically amplify utilisation. The health score distribution reveals that most patients self-report good health (score 0), yet even small deviations from perfect health correlate with measurably higher visit rates.
4 · Avg Visits by Illness Severity
A near-linear escalation: severity 0 → 0.079 visits; severity 5 → 0.814 visits. Each additional illness point raises visit probability by ~15 percentage points.
5 · % Patients Who Visited — by Illness Severity
At severity 0, only 6.8% of patients visited a doctor. By severity 5, that proportion rises to 41.9% — a 6× increase in the probability of seeking care.
6 · Chronic Condition Impact on Avg Visits
Limiting chronic conditions (lchronic) cause a +130% increase in visits over the baseline. Non-limiting chronic conditions still add a +27% uplift.
7 · Health Score Distribution (0 = Best Health)
58.3% of patients score 0 (excellent health). Scores above 6 are rare (<5%) but represent patients with significantly elevated visit rates and care complexity.
8 · Avg Visits Heatmap — Health Score Group × Illness Severity
The interaction between health score and illness severity reveals compounding risk. Patients with both high health scores (poor health) and high illness severity average 0.852 visits — the highest of any subgroup — while those with low health score and no illness barely exceed 0.129.
03 · Demographics — Gender, Age & Income
Demographic factors paint a clear picture of structural disparities in healthcare access and need. Women visit doctors 53% more often than men. Older patients visit nearly twice as frequently as the youngest cohort. Low-income patients visit 78% more than high-income patients, despite likely having fewer financial resources to afford care — suggesting unmet need is being driven by acute health necessity rather than discretionary utilisation.
9 · Avg Visits by Gender
Females average 0.362 visits vs 0.236 for males — a 53% gap. This aligns with global patterns of higher female healthcare-seeking behaviour.
10 · Health Profile Radar — Male vs Female
Female patients show higher scores across illness, health burden, reduced activity days, and visit rates. Males report higher income and are slightly younger on average.
11 · Avg Visits: Gender × Chronic Condition
Female patients with limiting chronic conditions average 0.694 visits — the highest of any gender-chronic subgroup. Males without any chronic condition average just 0.159.
12 · Avg Visits & Patient Count by Age Group
Visit rates follow an age-upward trend: the oldest cohort (scaled 0.72, approx. 65+) averages 0.483 visits — more than double the youngest groups. The dip at 0.47 may reflect a healthier mid-age subpopulation.
13 · Visit Rate & Avg Visits by Income
Low-income patients are 63% more likely to visit (25.6% vs 15.7%) and visit 78% more often on average. A clear socioeconomic gradient in utilisation exists.
04 · Insurance & Coverage Patterns
Insurance coverage reveals a nuanced and sometimes counterintuitive story. Private insurance holders do not visit more frequently than the uninsured — suggesting insurance type matters less than need. However, repatriation (freerepat) cardholders visit at nearly double the rate of those without, reflecting their older age profile and higher chronic disease burden. The free-poor scheme, surprisingly, shows the lowest visit rate — likely due to access barriers beyond cost.
14 · Insurance Coverage Mix
Only 44.3% hold private insurance. Repatriation (freerepat) covers 21% and free-poor just 4.3%. Many patients rely purely on public healthcare.
15 · % Patients Visiting by Insurance Type
Freerepat holders have the highest visit rate at 31.6%. Freepoor holders have the lowest at 9.0% — highlighting a major access barrier for the poorest patients.
16 · Avg Visits by Insurance
Freerepat average 0.467 vs freepoor at only 0.158. Private insurance shows no significant advantage over uninsured.
17 · Avg Visits — Gender × Private Insurance
Private insurance barely shifts male visit behaviour (0.237 vs 0.236), but for females, those without private insurance actually visit slightly more (0.380 vs 0.341) — suggesting need-driven utilisation dominates over insurance access effects.
05 · Activity, Wellbeing & Functional Impact
Activity-reduced days — the number of days a patient could not perform normal activities due to illness — serve as a powerful proxy for functional health burden. As days increase, visit frequency rises sharply, though with notable volatility at higher day counts (small sample sizes). Taken together with health scores, this paints a portrait of patients where functional impairment directly drives care-seeking behaviour.
18 · Activity-Reduced Days vs Avg Doctor Visits
Patients with 0 reduced days average just 0.184 visits. Those with 10+ days average 2.0–5.0 visits. The trend is strongly positive, with spikes at high day counts driven by small but severely ill subgroups.
19 · Illness Severity Distribution
The largest group (31.6%) reports mild illness (score 1). Nearly 30% report no illness. Severe illness (scores 4–5) affects 9.8% of the population but drives disproportionate utilisation.

🔍 Summary of Key Findings

Zero-Inflated Visit Pattern
79.8% of patients recorded zero doctor visits — a textbook zero-inflated count distribution. Standard OLS regression is inappropriate; zero-inflated Poisson or negative-binomial models are required for accurate modelling.
Illness Severity is the #1 Driver
Severity-5 patients average 0.814 visits — 10× more than severity-0 (0.079). Every unit increase in illness severity raises the probability of visiting a doctor by ~15 percentage points.
Chronic Conditions Compound Risk
Limiting chronic conditions raise visits by 130% over baseline. Non-limiting conditions still add 27%. When both health score and illness are high, patients average 0.852 visits — the highest subgroup.
Gender Disparity is Structural
Female patients visit 53% more often than males. This gap is consistent across all insurance types and income brackets, suggesting it reflects genuine behavioural and biological differences rather than access effects.
Income Inversely Predicts Visits
Low-income patients (<0.3) average 0.398 visits and are 63% more likely to visit than high-income patients — highlighting need-driven, not discretionary, utilisation among lower socioeconomic groups.
Age Raises Utilisation Monotonically
The oldest cohort (scaled 0.72 ≈ 65+) averages 0.483 visits — 2.4× more than the youngest cohort (0.201). Ageing populations will place growing demand on healthcare systems.
Activity Impairment Signals High Need
Patients with 10+ activity-reduced days average 2–5 visits. This metric is a strong proxy for functional burden and could serve as a screening flag for high-utilisation patients.
Freepoor Access Paradox
Despite being the most financially vulnerable group, freepoor holders have the lowest visit rate (9%) — suggesting non-financial barriers (geographic, cultural, awareness) remain even when cost is addressed.
