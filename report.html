Executive Summary

The Big Picture

This report analyses the Australian Health Survey dataset of 5,190 individuals, examining the factors that influence how often people visit a doctor. The 13 variables span demographics (age, gender, income), health status (illness severity, chronic conditions, self-rated health), functional impairment (activity-reduced days), and three distinct insurance coverage types.

The single most striking feature of the data is its zero-inflated distribution — 79.8% of respondents recorded zero doctor visits in the reference period. This is not statistical noise; it reflects a genuine behavioural story — one about whether people visit, and another about how often they visit once they cross that threshold.

The analysis that follows is structured around five core themes: health burden, demographics, insurance coverage, functional impairment, and modelling implications. Together, these themes tell a story not just about who uses healthcare, but more importantly, who does not — and why that absence matters for policy.

Section 1

The Paradox of Non-Utilisation

If you were to design a healthcare utilisation dataset and asked what the most common number of doctor visits should be, you might guess one or two. The reality is far more stark: in this dataset of 5,190 Australians, the answer is zero. Zero dominates the landscape — a pattern statisticians call zero inflation, and one that carries profound implications for how we model and understand healthcare demand.

79.8% of patients — 4,141 of 5,190 — recorded zero doctor visits.
The modal response in this healthcare dataset is no healthcare at all.
Visits Distribution
Overwhelmingly zero — 4,141 patients (79.8%) visited no doctor at all in the survey period.
Cumulative % of Patients (CDF)
94.9% of patients have ≤1 visit. Only 5% are "high-utilisation" patients with 2+ visits.

Among those who did visit, the mean was 1.04 visits — but this average masks the tail: a small group of patients (roughly 1%) recorded 5 or more visits, driving the overall mean above zero. The core analytical question is not "how many times did people visit?" but rather what crosses the threshold that separates zero visits from any visit at all?

This zero-inflation almost certainly reflects two distinct populations: a structural group who genuinely did not need medical attention during the reference period, and a latent group who needed care but did not access it — due to cost, geography, cultural barriers, or plain avoidance. This distinction — between voluntary non-use and involuntary non-use driven by barriers — is one of the most important in health equity research.

Section 2

When Illness Strikes, People Visit

Of all the variables in this dataset, illness severity has the clearest and most dramatic relationship with doctor visits. The survey's illness severity scale (0 = no illness, 5 = very ill) delivers perhaps the most unambiguous signal in the entire analysis: moving from perceived illness to maximum severity produces a more than ten-fold increase in average visits.

From 0.079 at severity 0 to 0.814 at severity 5 — a near-linear 10× escalation.

The healthcare system relies on patients to act on their health signals — and a significant proportion do not, even when those signals are loud.

Section 3

The Chronic Condition Burden

Beyond acute illness, the dataset captures two distinct types of chronic conditions: nchronic (non-limiting chronic conditions, such as controlled hypertension or managed asthma) and lchronic (limiting chronic conditions — those that restrict daily activities). The difference between these two categories in terms of healthcare impact is striking.

Non-limiting conditions add a significant but more modest +27% uplift.
Chronic Condition Impact on Avg Visits

Health Score Distribution (0 = Best)
58.3% report perfect health (score 0). Scores above 6 are rare but correlate with sharply elevated utilisation.
Avg Visits Heatmap — Health Score × Illness Severity
Compounding risk: patients with both high health scores (poor self-rated health) and severe illness average 0.852 visits — the highest of any subgroup.

The 130% increase associated with lchronic reflects the compounding demands of ongoing management: regular check-ups, medication reviews, specialist referrals, and coordinated care. By contrast, the 27% increase from nchronic still represents a meaningful signal — one that separates patients with background health complexity from those with a clean bill of health.

Together, these findings underscore the importance of chronic disease prevalence as a primary driver of healthcare demand forecasting. In an ageing population, the proportion of patients with limiting chronic conditions will only grow — and with it, the pressure on primary and specialist care services.

Section 4

Gender, Age & the Social Gradient

Three demographic variables — gender, age, and income — each independently shape healthcare utilisation in this dataset. Together, they define a structural gradient: older, lower-income women are the highest-utilisation subgroup, while younger, higher-income men are the lowest. These are not surprises, but the magnitude of the differences is worth examining carefully.

Avg Visits by Gender
Females average 0.362 visits vs 0.236 for males — a consistent 53% gap across all subgroups.
Male vs Female Health Profile (Radar)
Females score higher across illness burden, health score, reduced days, and visit rates. Males report higher income.

Gender: Female respondents averaged 0.362 visits compared to 0.236 for males — a gap of approximately 53%. This is consistent with international evidence showing higher female healthcare-seeking behaviour, but the data also reveals an important nuance: the gender gap is consistent across all insurance types and income brackets, suggesting it reflects genuine biological and behavioural differences rather than differential access.

Avg Visits & Patient Count by Age Group
A clear age-upward trend: the oldest cohort (scaled 0.72 ≈ 65+) averages 0.483 visits — 2.4× more than the youngest groups (0.201).

Age: The oldest cohort (65+) averaged 0.483 visits against 0.201 for the youngest group — a gradient of more than 2.4×. This is expected: older individuals accumulate chronic conditions, manage long-term medications, and require more routine monitoring. What is notable is that the relationship is not entirely linear — there is a slight dip around middle age (cohort 0.47) before the sharp rise in older groups, suggesting a particularly healthy mid-life subpopulation in this sample.

Healthcare utilisation decreases as income increases — an inverse social gradient driven by need, not choice.
Avg Visits & % Who Visited by Income Bracket
Low-income patients are 63% more likely to visit (25.6% vs 15.7%) and average 78% more visits than high-income patients.

Income: The income gradient runs counter to a simple "more money, more healthcare" narrative. Lower-income individuals visit the doctor more frequently on average — not because they are over-utilising, but because they carry a higher disease burden driven by worse living conditions, occupational risks, and reduced access to preventive care. This is a textbook inverse social gradient: the populations most in need are using the system more, but almost certainly still not enough given their relative health disadvantage.

Section 5

The Insurance Paradox

Few areas of the data are as counterintuitive as the insurance findings. The dataset contains three insurance-related variables — freerepat (government repat scheme for veterans and their families), freepoor (means-tested free care for the very poor), and private (voluntary private insurance) — and the patterns they reveal are both nuanced and policy-relevant.

% Patients Visiting — by Insurance Type
Freerepat holders visit most (31.6%). Freepoor holders visit least (9.0%) — paradoxically the most vulnerable group.
Avg Visits by Insurance Scheme
Freerepat: 0.467 avg visits. Freepoor: only 0.158. Private insurance shows no meaningful edge over uninsured.

Freerepat cardholders averaged 0.467 visits — the highest of any insurance group. This is not surprising once you consider the demographic profile of repatriation cardholders: predominantly older veterans and their families, with higher rates of chronic conditions and functional impairment. Their elevated utilisation is a reflection of genuine health need, not insurance-induced moral hazard.

The story for freepoor cardholders is starkly different. Despite being means-tested as the most financially vulnerable in the dataset, this group averaged only 0.158 visits — less than a third of the freerepat average — and only 9% of freepoor cardholders reported any visit at all. This is the insurance paradox in sharp relief: removing the financial barrier alone is clearly not sufficient to ensure utilisation. Geographic distance, cultural factors, distrust of institutions, or the sheer difficulty of navigating a healthcare system while managing poverty may all play a role.

Avg Visits — Gender × Private Insurance
Private insurance has negligible effect on males (0.237 vs 0.236). For females, those without private insurance visit slightly more (0.380 vs 0.341) — need dominates over coverage.

Perhaps most counterintuitively, private insurance showed essentially no meaningful effect on visit rates: privately insured individuals averaged 0.295 visits versus 0.307 for the uninsured — a negligible and likely statistically insignificant difference. This challenges the assumption that private insurance is a primary driver of healthcare utilisation. In a system with universal public coverage like Australia's, private insurance may offer choice and reduced waiting times, but it does not appear to meaningfully change the frequency of visits at a population level.

Section 6

Functional Impairment as a Warning Sign

One of the most practically useful variables in this dataset is the number of days on which a respondent was unable to perform their normal activities due to illness or injury — referred to here as activity-reduced days. This measure of functional impairment provides a more objective signal than self-reported illness severity, and its relationship with doctor visits is strongly positive.

Activity-Reduced Days vs Avg Doctor Visits
A near-monotonic rise: from 0.184 visits at 0 days to 2.0+ visits at 10 days. The spike at 11 days (5.0 visits) is a small-n outlier — treat with caution.

At zero activity-reduced days — the majority of respondents — average visits stand at just 0.184. As impairment increases, so do visits. By 10 days of reduced activity, average visits have climbed to 2.0 — roughly an eleven-fold increase. The relationship is not perfectly smooth — there are fluctuations driven by small subgroup sizes at higher day counts — but the directional signal is unambiguous.

One notable outlier: respondents reporting 11 activity-reduced days recorded a mean of 5.0 visits — a dramatic spike. Small n — treat with caution This figure should be interpreted carefully; with only 2 respondents in this bucket, the mean is highly unstable. However, it is consistent with the broader pattern: the most severely impaired patients are also the most frequent users of healthcare.

The practical implication is significant: activity-reduced days are a powerful leading indicator of healthcare demand. A monitoring system that tracked activity limitation in real time — through wearables, GP check-ins, or community health workers — could serve as an early-warning signal for individuals at risk of high utilisation, enabling proactive rather than reactive care delivery.

Section 7

Modelling Recommendations

The structure of this dataset makes standard linear regression entirely inappropriate. The outcome variable — number of doctor visits — is a non-negative integer with a massive spike at zero and a long but thin right tail. Applying OLS regression to such data would violate core assumptions, produce negative predicted values, and systematically misestimate the relationships between predictors and outcomes.

Three modelling frameworks are appropriate for this type of zero-inflated count data:

1. Zero-Inflated Poisson (ZIP) regression — Models the data as a mixture of two processes: a logistic model predicting structural zeros (those who would never visit), and a Poisson count model for those who might. This neatly separates the question of who visits from how often.

2. Zero-Inflated Negative Binomial (ZINB) regression — Preferred when the count component also exhibits overdispersion (variance exceeds the mean). Given the spread of visits in this dataset, ZINB is likely the most robust specification.

3. Hurdle models — An alternative two-part specification where the first part models the binary outcome (any visit vs none) and the second part models the count given at least one visit. Hurdle models are simpler to interpret and avoid assumptions about the source of zeros.

Conclusion

From Data to Policy

This dataset tells a story of a healthcare system under two simultaneous pressures: over-utilisation by the most ill (the small minority who visit frequently due to chronic conditions, severe illness, and functional impairment) and under-utilisation by the most vulnerable (the freepoor cardholders, low-income groups, and structurally absent zero-visitors who likely need care but are not getting it). The average visit rate of 0.302 obscures this bimodal reality almost entirely — which is precisely why statistical methods designed for zero-inflated distributions, rather than simple averages, are essential for sound healthcare planning.

The policy implications are clear. Reducing the zero-visit rate among high-risk groups — those with limiting chronic conditions, elevated illness severity, functional impairment, older age, or low income — requires interventions that go beyond making healthcare free. The freepoor paradox is the starkest illustration of this: cost coverage exists, but utilisation remains the lowest of any group. Outreach, health literacy, community health workers, and reducing structural barriers must accompany financial subsidies. The data demands a shift from simply providing healthcare to actively connecting people to it — meeting patients where they are, not waiting for them to arrive.
